DIY Brown Noise Masking Machine: Sounds for Larger and Smaller Speakers and Headphones

Published on July 11, 2026 by Helmut

In this post, I am sharing a family of optimized brown noises with different roll-off frequencies (30 to 100 Hz).

I made these sounds for covering (masking) annoying and startling low and mid frequency noises I might encounter at night. Think of traffic noise, an upstairs neighbor, barking dogs, chatter from an adjacent room, etc. At times, I also play them for relaxation during the day.

You’ll find the download link (a zip archive) further down, once we’ve walked through the why and how.

These masking sounds can be played via speakers or headphones and are independent of any particular white noise app (can be discontinued, become a subscription, etc.) or white noise machine (limited by the speaker size and design constraints).

I have now used them for several months at night, mostly via a medium-large party speaker, and while traveling via a more portable Bluetooth speaker and noise canceling earbuds.

Why a brown noise ladder with different roll-off frequencies?

I wanted to max out the ability of my speakers and headphones (all with different abilities to reproduce lower frequencies) to mask incidental lower frequency noises (footfalls, traffic, etc.).

Importantly, the maskers are all filtered brown noises (-6 dB per octave) so they sound very similar. They primarily differ in the amount of low end / rumble they can produce, given a suitable speaker.

Here is an illustration of the brown noise ladder (the lower/higher the roll-off, the more/less rumble):

Unlike with many white noise machines, I don’t have to resort to other noise colors (e.g. pink, white…) to increase/decrease the amount of low end in my masking noise.

Initially I tested these masking sounds with headphones that have a full frequency range to find out which roll-off frequency would be best suited for masking the footfalls from my upstairs neighbors.

Somewhat surprisingly, the 60 Hz masker worked best and maskers up to 80 Hz were nearly as good. (Tiled concrete floor. Neighbor presumably walking on their heels.)

Looking at the spectrum, I expected having to go down to 40 Hz, but it turned out that most of the disturbance was covered by choosing one of the 60, 70, and 80 Hz maskers.
(Depending on your ceiling and speaker, you might have to go lower or get away with a higher roll-off)
My preference for nighttime use is to go with as little rumble as possible, so I would choose 80 Hz in this scenario.

If you are interested in dealing with heavy walking noise in particular, also read my post How to Block Loud Footstep Noise from an Upstairs Apartment.

Removing hiss

While less so than pink noise, even brown noise contains a bit of hiss. I don’t mind this at lower volumes during the day. It gives the sound a somewhat lighter flavor.

However, at night and at higher volumes, I find the hiss somewhat startling. It makes it harder for me to fall asleep.

For this reason, I have rolled off the treble frequencies in the sound files, starting from 4500 Hz. This makes the brown noise less alerting. The filtering somewhat reduces the masking effectiveness against high frequency noise, but doors, walls, and ceilings typically already reduce higher frequency components coming from outside quite well.

My current setup

At home, I am currently using a medium-large Bluetooth party speaker, the JBL Encore Essential 2, which I had purchased because of its substantial power and good low frequency extension for its size (40 Hz to 20 kHz, according to JBL). In my own tests the Encore 2 does indeed produce decent levels from 40 Hz and shines from 45 Hz.

Note: the Encore is a rugged party/PA-style speaker (6.2 kg, 13.67 lbs) often used outdoors and played at louder volumes. If outdoors / party does not resonate with you, for bedroom use, an indoor hi-fi speaker might serve you better.

During a recent trip, I used the much smaller DOSS Soundbox (100 Hz to 18 kHz) with the 100-Hz masker and was very satisfied with the results: I got a mellow sound and good masking of the hotel room fridge and other incidental noises coming from the hallway.

The Soundbox can’t properly reproduce the lower bass frequencies present in full-spectrum brown noise or even filtered noise with a 60 Hz roll-off. Consequently, going much lower than 100 Hz doesn’t help with masking and can lead to distortions at higher volumes.

Note: I have had the Soundbox for a several years. Some newer compact Bluetooth speakers state a substantially better LFE, e.g. 70 Hz.

How can you play these brown noises?

Start with one of the speakers or headphones you already have. This could be a compact Bluetooth speaker, a bookshelf speaker, a sound system, a subwoofer, etc.

The lower the frequency of the sounds you want to mask, the more low-end your speakers will have to deliver, but for distant traffic, chatter coming from the hallway, or a dog barking at a distance, a decent smaller speaker with a low frequency extension down to about 100 Hz can go a long way.

Even if you have a large speaker, I would start with 80 to 100 Hz and only go as low as you have to.

The lower the roll-off frequency, the more rumble the sound has. That rumble is good for masking incidental low frequency sounds that would otherwise wake you up or startle you as you are falling asleep, but the rumble itself can become a disturbance as well.

Which sound player works well?

Each file is about 5 minutes long. I have cross-faded the sounds so that they can be repeated seamlessly. Having used the brown noises now for several months, I have not noticed any repetition, clicks, or patterns in the sounds.

All sounds are in ogg format. Compared to lossless formats, this saves quite a bit of space while still delivering very good sound quality.

To play these files continuously, you need a modern sound player that can play ogg files and loop without introducing gaps.

Note: Why not mp3? The mp3 format does not allow for gap-less looping (you would hear a click or similar) even when the files are perfectly cross-faded, which makes it unsuitable for continuous playback.

Personally, I like Foobar 2000 as a sound player, in particular for playing masking sounds. It is free and available for Android, iPhone and iPad, Mac, and Windows.

Foobar 2000 has an excellent playback engine that can repeat sound-files designed for continuous playback without introducing any gaps whatsoever, and it is one of very few free options on iOS that has a built-in multi-band equalizer, allowing you to tweak the sounds further.

Note: Unlike some Android variants, iOS does not have a built-in multi-band EQ and most third-party players that have one cost quite a bit of money.

Downloading the files

Please feel free to use these files for yourself and your family. If you want to let friends or others know about them, share a link to this post rather than the files themselves.

For anything else — distributing the files, putting them on other sites, selling them, or making them part of an app — contact me first.

Install Foobar 2000 first (available in Google Play [Android] and App Store [iOS]).

Then download the files. Here is the download link to the zip archive: Brown Noise Maskers vol 1

On both Android and iOS I recommend downloading and saving the zip archive in the folder Downloads and extracting it there. As a result you get the brown noise ladder as a folder.

On iPhone and iPad, move that folder into the foobar2000 folder (via the files app).

On Android, move the extracted brown noise folder directly into the standard music folder.

Foobar 2000 tweaks

Seamless continuous play of single tracks
To enable repeat for single tracks, you need to change a setting available under the cog wheel:

Change Playback Order to One Track and make sure Repeat is toggled on:

This ensures that your selected brown noise track is played continuously until you stop it.

There should be no gap and no perception of looping.

Using the multiband EQ to customize the sound (optional)

The EQ can be found under DSP Manager (settings):

Printed in blue are the settings I use when I want to preserve the character of brown noise to relax and mask everyday noises but no rumble whatsoever. Try this with the 100 Hz masker. Only the sliders from 55 to 156 Hz need to be changed. The rest remain at 0.

By toggling the EQ on and off you can choose between more “masking rumble” and relaxed mode.

Final thoughts: how to get the most out of these sounds

You can use these brown noises in several ways.

At night, you could play them at a moderate volume to mask many everyday incidental noises, such as creaking, a fridge turning on, dogs, chatter, or traffic at a distance, etc.

I also find them helpful to make the bedroom not too quiet, and I have used them for relaxation during the day.

To shield yourself from louder sudden noises, you likely will have to play the files quite loud. A dog barking outside the window, a garbage truck directly passing by, and a heavy upstairs neighbor aren’t going to fade away against a subtle brown noise background.

To make loud masking noise work in such scenarios, I very often wear earplugs and then increase the masking volume so that I can hear it well but it doesn’t annoy me. I will even go to a slight annoyance level if necessary.
Without earplugs, the same volume would be way too much. Keep this in mind if you have other people sleeping in the same room.

A third, very effective way to use these brown noises is via noise cancelling headphones or earbuds. That way you get to reduce even loud external noise first via the headphones’ ANC and isolation, and then play brown noise at a moderate volume to cover the remainder.

Let me know in the comments how it goes.
